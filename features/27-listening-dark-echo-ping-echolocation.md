# 27. The Listening Dark — Echo-Ping Echolocation

> Fire a silent pulse into the dark and the room answers — a fan of delayed clicks whose timing, pan and pitch tell you how far, which way, and whether you hit stone, lava, or something that breathes.

**The idea** — A new always-available, ammo-free ability bound alongside the Whip: tap it and the player emits a *silent* ping. The engine casts a horizontal-and-slight-vertical fan of rays out from the eye, and for each ray that connects it schedules a short click to play after a delay proportional to hit distance. Near surfaces tick back almost instantly; the far wall answers half a second later. Each click is **panned** left/right by the ray's yaw offset and **pitched/timbred** by what it struck: a dry woody knock for brushwork, a wet sizzling chirp for lava and toxic sludge, and a soft fleshy thud — slightly warbling — for a monster rig. In pitch black, a ping repaints the geometry as a spreading constellation of sound.

**Why it's fresh** — Echolocation exists in stealth games as a screen-space wall-hack overlay. This is the opposite: nothing renders. The information lives entirely in *time, stereo and timbre*, so reading a room becomes an ear skill, not a glance at a recolored mesh. It turns QUAKECLONE's existing dark-fog atmosphere from a handicap into a medium you actively interrogate.

**How it plays** — Walk into a fog-choked corridor, ping, and count: two fast clicks hard-left mean a near wall and an alcove; a delayed wet sizzle dead-ahead is a lava pit you were about to stroll into; a warbling flesh-click drifting right is an Ogre that hasn't seen you. Pinging costs nothing but noise discipline — it's loud to *you*, not the monsters, so it rewards stopping, listening, and committing to a blind rocket down a bearing you only heard.

**How it fits QUAKECLONE** — It leans directly on `physics.rs` `raycast_world`, which already returns `(distance, point, normal)` against the same `&[Aabb]` solids list — including the per-frame moving colliders the doors and `vehicle.rs` truck rewrite, so a ping naturally bounces off a passing truck. Material classification reuses the surface tagging that `level.rs`'s Build API and hazard styling assign per brush; monster hits resolve through the `enemies.rs` collider entities and `monster_model.rs` rigs. Click synthesis is a handful of new entries in `audio_gen.rs` (its `noise`/`sine_sweep`/`buzz` primitives already make exactly this kind of short transient), played and delay-scheduled through `audio.rs`.

**Build sketch** — A ping system fires N rays in a fan, collects hits, and pushes timed `(delay, pan, click_kind)` events into a small scheduler resource drained each frame. The honest hard part: the `Aabb` solids list carries *geometry, not material*. Today a raycast knows where it hit but not what — so the real work is threading a lightweight material/owner tag alongside each collider (brush surface kind, hazard, or monster entity) without bloating the hot collision path the swept solver shares. Stereo pan needs the hit bearing relative to view yaw; getting density right (enough rays to read a room, few enough to stay legible) is tuning, not architecture.

**Effort** — **M.** Main risk: it's a perception feature, so it lives or dies on audio tuning — if the clicks don't *read* as a legible map of distance and direction, it's just clutter. Plumbing the material tag through the collider list is the one structural change worth doing carefully.
